# Pretext

Most people purchase cameras for security.

You have: 
- Personal security (i.e.; in your home)
- Corporate Security (i.e; in a work environment)
- everything else, aka "boring cameras" (i.e; a intended public community camera watching a zoo exhibit or a museum item, designed to be accessible to the public)

With cameras, you mainly have 2 types:
1. DVR Cameras
2. IP Cameras

### DVR
DVR cameras run over analog systems and store their live feed/footage to a DVR recorder, which is a physical onsite device.

These cameras are still very much prevalent in todays world, and you can often times find these camera systems in locations where price and simplicity in older infrastructures matter more than upgrading.

Examples are: 
1. Gas stations 
2. Small retail stores
3. Bars

Essentially, locations where not a whole lot of effort is placed into security, and often times don't have the budget/team size to invest into their security.

DVR has a lower upfront cost, a simple "plug and play" setup in contrast to IP, no reliance on networking, and is good enough for many people.

DVR is gradually being phased out, and more common in legacy systems/budget setups.

### IP
IP cameras run over the internet and store their live feed/footage to a server in the cloud.

These cameras are what you're likely used to, such as your Ring doorbell.

You can find IP cameras in most modern security systems, such as: 
1. Hospitals and healthcare facilities
2. Schools and Universities 
3. Hotels and airports
4. Homes and modern businesses

While you can obviously find IP cameras in gas stations and bars, or DVR cameras in hotels, homes and modern businesses... the above is true for majority listed. Exceptions don't disprove rules, although they're becoming less with time.

This post focuses solely on IP cameras, as most DVR systems run locally off the internet and can't be accessed from my desk.

### Most are unaware their IP camera is public

If you get into the hobby of camera dorking, you will quickly realize that many of the cameras you discover are definitely NOT supposed to be publicly accessible. This plays a huge role into today's story.

IP cameras are safe if you use them properly. However, you will find many cameras in office buildings where the building IT guy set the camera up with terrible access security (aka the feed is literally not password protected).

You also have people who buy cameras and install them in their homes really fast as a quick "set it and forget it" procedure. Or... maybe even a grandpa assigns the task of installing his cameras to his grandson.

Essentially, IP cameras are great if they require an app where you have to create an account to view the feed. This applies to Ring, SimpliSafe, Arlo, etc.

Where it gets drastically LESS secure is when you purchase a camera that broadcasts its feed to a local web server that you can view on a web browser, either on your phone or computer. This is where the feed eventually gets crawled and indexed by Google
